Low-light photography is difficult because less light reaches the camera. To
make a usable exposure, the camera or photographer usually has to collect
light for longer, admit more light through the lens, amplify the recorded
signal more, or combine those responses. Each choice changes the picture as
well as its brightness.[^nikon-low-light]

The exposure relationships are explained in
[the Exposure fundamentals guide](exposure-fundamentals.md). In low light, the
practical question is which compromise matters least for the intended picture.

## The three main trade-offs

### Slower shutter speed

A longer exposure gathers more light, but any camera movement during that time
can blur the whole image. A moving subject can also blur even when the camera
is perfectly supported. These are separate problems: stabilizing the camera
does not freeze a person, animal or vehicle that moves during the exposure.
[^sony-shutter-speed]

### Wider aperture

A wider aperture admits more light and may permit a shorter exposure or lower
ISO. It also makes depth of field shallower, so less of the scene may appear
acceptably sharp. The device may not provide direct aperture control; available
controls and automation vary.[^nikon-low-light]

### Higher ISO

A higher ISO can support a shorter shutter speed when light is scarce, helping
reduce blur from camera shake or subject movement. The trade-off is greater
noise and potentially less fine detail. The useful balance varies with the
device and the intended output, so there is no universal best ISO.
[^nikon-low-light]

## Stabilize the device

When the subject is still, stabilization makes a longer exposure more usable.
Hold the device steadily, brace against a solid object when appropriate, or
place it on a stable support. A tripod is useful when the exposure becomes too
long to hold reliably. Avoid unnecessary movement while the photograph is
being recorded.[^nikon-low-light][^sony-shutter-speed]

Stabilization features and their behavior on a tripod vary by device and lens.
Consult the manufacturer's documentation rather than assuming the same setting
is correct everywhere.

## Account for subject movement

A tripod can keep a building sharp but cannot keep a walking person sharp. If
the subject is moving and should appear frozen, the exposure must be short
enough for that movement; raising ISO or admitting more light may be needed to
support it. If blur is intentional, a longer exposure can instead communicate
movement.[^nikon-low-light][^sony-shutter-speed]

## Protect the important tones

Night scenes often contain very bright lamps or signs beside deep shadows. An
automatic exposure may not match how the scene looked or which detail matters:
the sky can become too bright, lights can lose color and detail, or darker
areas can become nearly black.[^sony-night-views]

Make an initial photograph, inspect the result, then adjust the exposure toward
the intended balance where the device permits it. Decide which highlights must
retain detail and which dark areas are important; a night photograph does not
need every shadow to look like daylight. Exposure compensation may help when
an automatic result is consistently too bright or too dark.
[^sony-night-views]

## Focus in low light

Autofocus depends on usable light and contrast, so a dark or featureless
subject can cause hunting or focus on the wrong distance. Where possible,
place the focus area on a clear edge or textured detail at the intended subject
distance. Check that the important subject—not a bright background light—is
the part that appears sharp.[^cambridge-autofocus]

## Check and repeat

Low-light results are hard to judge before capture. Make a test frame, review
brightness and sharpness, and take more than one photograph when the moment
allows. Recheck after changing support, subject distance or lighting. Small
differences in camera movement, subject movement and focus can produce very
different results.[^nikon-low-light][^sony-night-views]

Some devices provide automated low-light or night modes, and the way they
combine settings or frames varies. This guide does not assume those modes
exist and does not provide model-specific instructions.

Astrophotography, star trails, advanced flash systems, exposure stacking,
professional high-dynamic-range workflows and advanced editing are outside
this fundamentals document.
