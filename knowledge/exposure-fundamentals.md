Photographic exposure describes how light or dark a captured image appears.
An underexposed image receives too little exposure for the intended result and
appears too dark; an overexposed image receives too much and appears too
bright. Aperture, shutter speed and ISO work together to determine that
result.[^cambridge-camera-exposure]

These ideas apply whether a device gives you direct control or handles some or
all of them automatically. Available controls, exposure modes and control names
vary by device. Understanding the relationships still helps you understand the
choices the device is making.[^cambridge-camera-exposure]

## The three parts of exposure

### Aperture: the size of the opening

Aperture is the opening through which light passes in the lens. A wider opening
admits more light; a narrower opening admits less. Aperture is commonly written
as an f-number, and the numbering is inverse: a smaller f-number represents a
wider opening, while a larger f-number represents a narrower opening.[^nikon-exposure-basics]

Aperture also changes depth of field, the range of distances that appear in
focus. A wider aperture tends to produce a shallower depth of field. A narrower
aperture tends to produce a larger depth of field.[^cambridge-camera-exposure]

The creative question is therefore not only "How much light?" It is also "How
much of the scene should appear in focus?"

### Shutter speed: how long light is collected

Shutter speed, also called exposure time, is the length of time light is
allowed to reach the recording medium. A shorter exposure time admits less
light; a longer exposure time admits more.[^cambridge-camera-exposure]

It also changes how motion appears. A shorter exposure can make movement look
more frozen, while a longer exposure can record movement as blur. Blur can be a
mistake or a deliberate creative choice, depending on the intended
image.[^nikon-exposure-basics]

### ISO: the sensitivity setting

The approved sources describe ISO as the camera's sensitivity setting. A lower
ISO requires more light for a comparable exposure; a higher ISO requires less.
The trade-off is image quality: raising ISO tends to increase visible noise and
can reduce color quality.[^nikon-exposure-basics]

ISO can therefore help when the desired aperture and shutter speed would not
otherwise produce the needed brightness, but it is not a free increase in
brightness. The added noise is part of the decision.[^cambridge-camera-exposure]

## One exposure, different creative results

The three controls are linked. Many combinations can produce comparable image
brightness, but the photographs may not look the same because each control has
a different creative effect.[^cambridge-camera-exposure]

For example, widening the aperture admits more light and can make depth of
field shallower. Shortening the exposure time admits less light and can reduce
motion blur. Changing both in opposite directions can preserve comparable
brightness while changing how focus and motion appear. Raising ISO can also
support a shorter exposure time or narrower aperture, with the trade-off of
more image noise.[^nikon-exposure-basics]

A useful mental model is:

- aperture trades light against depth of field;
- shutter speed trades light against the appearance of motion;
- ISO trades required light against noise and image quality.

There is no single combination that is creatively correct for every
photograph. The intended depth of field, treatment of motion and acceptable
image quality guide the balance.[^nikon-exposure-basics]

## Stops: a way to compare changes

A stop is a relative unit for exposure. A change of one full stop means twice
as much light or half as much light. This gives the three controls a shared way
to describe equivalent changes.[^nikon-exposure-basics]

If one control reduces exposure by one stop, an opposite one-stop change in
another control can restore comparable brightness. The creative side effects
still change: depth of field, motion rendering or noise may be different even
when overall brightness is similar.[^cambridge-camera-exposure]

## Basic camera metering

A camera's built-in meter measures light reflected from the scene and uses
that measurement to estimate an exposure. Because subjects reflect different
amounts of light, the meter has to make an assumption about what a typical tone
should look like. Very bright or very dark subjects can therefore lead the
meter toward an exposure that makes the subject look more like a middle
tone.[^cambridge-metering]

Metering systems may evaluate a broad area of the scene or give more weight to
a smaller region. The available choices and the way they are selected vary by
device. Whatever method is used, the meter is a starting estimate rather than
proof that the result matches the photographer's intention.[^cambridge-metering]

## Basic exposure compensation

Exposure compensation shifts the exposure target produced by the metering
system. Increasing compensation asks the automated exposure system for a
brighter result; decreasing it asks for a darker result. This is useful when a
reflective-light meter is consistently making a bright subject too dark or a
dark subject too bright.[^cambridge-metering]

Exposure compensation does not replace the relationships among aperture,
shutter speed and ISO. It tells the automated system to aim above or below its
original metered result. Whether this control is available, and how it affects
the settings chosen, depends on the device and exposure mode.[^cambridge-metering]

## What to remember

Exposure is a balance, not three separate decisions. Begin with the creative
result you care about most: depth of field, the appearance of motion or image
quality. The other controls, whether chosen by you or by the device, complete
the exposure. Different combinations can reach comparable brightness while
producing visibly different photographs.[^cambridge-camera-exposure]
