Focus determines which subject distance is rendered most sharply. Light from
an in-focus point converges at the camera's recording surface; light from an
out-of-focus point spreads out and appears blurred. Sharpness changes
gradually in front of and behind the focus distance rather than ending at a
perfect boundary.[^cambridge-depth-of-field]

A useful mental model is: **focus chooses the anchor of sharpness; depth of
field describes how much around that anchor also appears acceptably sharp.**
The aperture relationship is covered in
[the Exposure fundamentals guide](exposure-fundamentals.md).

## Autofocus basics

Autofocus adjusts the lens to seek sharpness at the selected part of the
scene. Many systems judge focus from contrast, so a clear edge or visible
texture is generally easier to focus on than a plain, low-contrast surface.
Low light, low subject contrast and subject or camera movement can all make
autofocus less reliable.[^cambridge-autofocus]

The photographer still has an important decision: tell the device which
subject matters. If the intended person is in front of a detailed background,
for example, an automatic system may focus on the background unless the focus
area is placed on the person.

## Single focus and continuous focus

Two common autofocus behaviors are:

- **Single or one-shot focus:** the device focuses for a still subject and
  then holds that focus for the photograph.
- **Continuous focus:** the device keeps adjusting focus while a moving
  subject changes distance.

Single focus is a natural starting point for subjects that stay at the same
distance. Continuous focus is intended for moving subjects, because a focus
distance chosen earlier may be wrong by the time the photograph is made.
Manufacturers use names such as one-shot, single autofocus, continuous
autofocus and servo autofocus for related behaviors; the exact names and
operation vary by device.[^cambridge-autofocus][^sony-af-modes]

## Focus points and focus areas

A focus point or focus frame identifies a place where the system should seek
focus. A small selectable area gives the photographer precise control over
which detail matters. A wider or automatic area lets the device choose from a
larger part of the frame. Neither is universally better: precision helps when
the subject is small or surrounded by distractions, while a broader area can
make an erratically moving subject easier to keep within the focusing region.
[^nikon-photography-glossary]

Subject tracking builds on continuous focus. After the photographer or device
identifies a subject, the system attempts to follow it across available focus
areas and maintain focus as it moves. Tracking helps, but it does not guarantee
a sharp result; the subject can leave the active area, become hidden, lack
contrast or move in a way the system cannot follow.[^nikon-photography-glossary]

## Focusing on moving subjects

Where the device offers the controls, a beginner can:

1. choose continuous or tracking focus for a subject whose distance is
   changing;
2. place the active focus area on the important part of the subject;
3. begin focusing before the key moment, then keep the subject within that
   area while making the photograph; and
4. check the result and try again if the important detail is not sharp.

For a subject following a predictable path, another option is to focus in
advance at the place where the subject is expected to arrive, then make the
photograph when it reaches that area.[^cambridge-autofocus]

## Foreground, subject and background

Focus directs attention. Focusing on a nearby object can make the background
less distinct; focusing farther away can leave a close foreground soft. How
much of both regions appears acceptably sharp depends on depth of field, not on
focus alone.[^cambridge-depth-of-field]

Before taking the picture, ask: **What must be sharp for this photograph to
work?** Put the focus anchor there, then decide whether the foreground and
background should support the subject sharply or fall away softly.

## Common beginner mistakes

- **Letting the device choose the wrong subject.** Place the focus area on the
  intended subject rather than assuming the nearest or most detailed object is
  the right one.
- **Using held focus after the distance changes.** Refocus when either the
  subject or camera has moved closer or farther.
- **Using single focus for continuing movement.** Use continuous focus or a
  planned pre-focus point when available and appropriate.
- **Choosing a featureless focus target.** Try a clear edge or texture at the
  same distance as the intended subject when autofocus struggles.

Phones and dedicated cameras differ in how much focus control they expose.
Some choose points and track subjects automatically; others offer selectable
modes and areas. Learn the concepts first, then consult the device's own
documentation for the controls it actually provides.
