Composition is how the photographer chooses what appears inside the frame and
how those elements relate to one another. It starts with a simple question:
**What is the main subject?** Once that is clear, placement, space, lines and
background can help the viewer recognize it.[^nikon-composition-guidelines]

Composition principles are tools, not laws. They offer useful starting points,
but an exception can serve a photograph better. Make a deliberate choice, then
compare alternatives rather than following a formula automatically.
[^nikon-composition-guidelines]

## Choose and simplify

Decide what made you want to take the photograph. Then scan the frame for
objects, bright patches or details that compete with it. Moving closer,
changing viewpoint, choosing a tighter frame or waiting for a distraction to
leave can simplify the scene. A change of angle can also place the subject
against a cleaner background.[^nikon-composing-photographs]

Framing is therefore both inclusion and exclusion. The edge of the photograph
should feel intentional: include elements that explain or support the subject,
and remove elements that pull attention away without adding meaning.

## Rule of thirds

The rule of thirds imagines a grid dividing the frame into thirds across and
down. Placing an important subject near a line or intersection can create an
off-center, balanced arrangement. The subject does not have to sit exactly on
an intersection, and a centered composition may work when symmetry,
reflection or a strong central subject calls for it.[^nikon-composition-guidelines]

Use the grid as a question rather than an order: **Does the subject feel
stronger here, in the center, or somewhere between?** Make more than one frame
when the answer is not obvious.

## Subject placement and visual balance

Placement changes how the empty and occupied parts of the frame feel. A person
or animal looking to one side often benefits from space in the direction of
its gaze. A moving subject can similarly have room in front of its direction
of travel.[^nikon-composition-guidelines]

Balance does not require matching objects on both sides. Compare the subject's
placement with the open space around it, and check whether another part of the
frame pulls attention so strongly that the intended subject becomes secondary.
[^nikon-composition-guidelines]

## Leading lines and framing elements

Roads, paths, walls, shadows, curves and repeated edges can guide the viewer's
eye through the frame or toward the subject. A leading line can also become the
subject itself. Check where the line enters, where it leads and whether it
supports the intended point of interest or carries attention out of the image.
[^nikon-composition-guidelines]

Objects near the edge—such as an opening, branch or doorway—can create a frame
within the photograph. Use this only when it clarifies the subject; an
attention-grabbing foreground frame can become a distraction instead.
[^nikon-center-composition]

## Background awareness

The background is part of the composition even when it is out of focus. Before
releasing the shutter, look around the subject for bright shapes, mergers at
the edges and clutter. Changing distance or viewpoint can reduce those
distractions. Differences in brightness, color or sharpness can also help the
subject separate from the background.[^nikon-composing-photographs]

## Negative space

Negative space is a relatively quiet area around the subject. It can make a
small subject more noticeable, create a sense of scale or give a facing or
moving subject room within the frame. Empty space is useful when it contributes
to the visual idea; it is not automatically wasted space.[^nikon-composing-photographs]

## Viewpoint and perspective

The first viewpoint is not always the clearest one. Walk around the subject
when it is safe, try a higher or lower position, and compare wider and tighter
framing. Changes in angle and distance alter which objects overlap, how much
background appears and which shapes dominate.[^nikon-composing-photographs]

## A practical decision sequence

Before making the photograph:

1. name the main subject;
2. choose what should remain inside the frame;
3. place the subject and decide how much space it needs;
4. use lines, framing or viewpoint only when they support that subject;
5. scan the background and every edge for distractions; and
6. try another composition when time allows.

The strongest habit is not memorizing rules. It is noticing the whole frame
before taking the picture, then learning from the alternatives.
